The monsoon arrives in Delhi in the last days of June, and the city changes almost overnight. For weeks the air has been dry and heavy, and the afternoons are too hot for anyone to walk outside. Then the clouds gather in the west, the wind turns cool, and the first rain falls on the dusty streets. Children run out of their houses to stand in it, and for a few minutes the whole neighbourhood smells of wet earth.

The rain does not stay pleasant for long. Within a week, the drains in many parts of the city are full, and roads that were empty a few days earlier are covered in dirty water. Traffic slows to a crawl, and people who live far from their offices leave much earlier than usual. Even so, most residents say they would rather have the monsoon than the heat that comes before it.

In the old parts of the city, the buildings tell a long story about weather. Thick stone walls keep the rooms cool in summer, and the narrow lanes give shade for most of the day. Newer buildings made of glass and steel look impressive, but they depend on air conditioning to be comfortable, and that uses a great deal of electricity. Engineers and architects have started to ask whether the older designs have lessons that are worth learning again.

Farmers outside the city watch the sky with even more attention. A late monsoon can delay planting, and a weak one can damage the harvest for the entire year. The price of vegetables in the market rises quickly when the rains fail, and families who spend most of their income on food feel the change first. For this reason, the monsoon is discussed in every home, every shop, and every newspaper from June until September.
